# Style guidelines for writing in English

This guide is intended as a reference for anyone writing in English at ECMWF. It aims to give our writing a unified appearance and should be seen as a set of guidelines rather than rigid rules.

Where variants in spelling or usage are possible, please refer to the Concise Oxford English Dictionary. See also the [OED online](https://www.oed.com/).

Part 1, [Writing style and word choice](#writing-style-and-word-choice), contains standard grammatical, stylistic, and word rules.

Part 2, [Style guidelines](#style-guidelines), details the options we have chosen for cases where there is more than one correct way to write something, such as dates, monetary amounts and headings.

## Writing style and word choice

Remember that English may not be the first language of many of our readers. It helps to vary the choice of words and avoid using words that are likely to be unfamiliar to the reader.

Aim to keep sentences simple and to express one idea per sentence. You can break long, complex sentences into shorter sentences or arrange information in lists or tables.

Bear in mind also that some documents written in English will be translated into other languages. Try to keep the text as uncluttered as possible, with a minimum of italics, bold, underlining and other such embellishments.

### Nouns and verbs

Using a lot of nouns can make sentences heavy and difficult to read. Often, you can make the sentence more concise by using verbs instead of nouns.

| Verbs | Nouns |
| --- | --- |
| The system **will be upgraded**. <br/> We **will upgrade** the system. | The **upgrade** of the system will be implemented. |
| We **have tightened** our production processes further. | We have implemented **a further tightening** of our production processes. |
| Supporting documentation **was examined**. | An **examination** of supporting documentation was carried out. |

### If constructions

In conditional sentences, try to express the condition first, then the effect.

| ✅ | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| If these standards are introduced, the accounting system will have to be modified. | The accounting system will have to be modified if these standards are introduced. |

### Sentence structure

Aim for an average sentence length of 20 to 25 words. Short sentences are easier to read and less likely to contain mistakes. Also try to vary the length of sentences and avoid several successive sentences having the same structure (such as a sequence of sentences starting with “The”).

Give your sentences a logical structure so that readers have an idea of the context before you give new information.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| At its meeting in June, the Council decided … |
| Since the lifts are old and no longer in production, we will need to replace them. |

### Active and passive

Use the active voice where possible (A does B, not B is done by A). This makes the information clearer and reduces the risk of ambiguity.

| ✅ Active | ❌ Passive |
| --- | --- |
| We will consider the matter shortly. | The matter will be considered shortly. |
| The Board decided to … | The decision was taken to … |

There are some cases where the passive voice is more appropriate. For example, if we do not know the subject of the sentence or if the subject is not important. Note also that in technical and scientific reports, the passive voice is usual.

| ✅ Passive | Note |
| --- | --- |
| Building work on the new office building was completed in the summer. | If the important information is that the building work has finished, it is not necessarily relevant to say who completed the work. |
| The performance of the ECMWF operational forecast model is reviewed. | Scientific report <br/> Alternative: This report reviews the performance of the ECMWF operational forecast model. |

Even if you decide the passive is more appropriate, take care not to bury the information in dense noun phrases. If you find yourself writing “something has been performed” or “something has been carried out”, consider whether you can make a verb out of the “something” (the noun), e.g. verification -> verify, assessment -> assess.

| Simplify this ... | To ... |
| --- | --- |
| **Verification of the EFI has been performed** using synoptic observations over Europe from the GTS. | **The EFI has been verified** using synoptic observations over Europe from the GTS. |
| An **assessment** of ECMWF’s existing tools for web data access and retrieval **has been carried out.** | ECMWF’s existing tools for web data access and retrieval **have been assessed**. |

### Commas in relative clauses

Use commas to separate non-defining clauses (information that could equally be placed in brackets). Defining clauses do not need commas. Use **that** in defining clauses and **which** in non-defining clauses (“*that* defines, *which* informs”).

| Defining | Non-defining |
| --- | --- |
| The computer that was purchased in 2000 must be replaced. | The computer, which was purchased in 2000, must be replaced. |

In the first case, several computers exist and we need to define for the reader which computer is meant = the one that was bought in 2000. In the second case, there is only one computer, or readers already know which computer is meant. The information in commas could be removed or placed in brackets and the sentence would still make sense.

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Staff members who contribute 50% will be entitled to claim. | Staff members, who contribute 50%, will be entitled to claim. |

In the first case, some staff members contribute 50%, some don’t. We need to define who is entitled to claim = those who contribute 50%. In the second case, all staff members contribute 50% and all will be entitled to claim. The part inside the commas is additional information that is not crucial to the meaning of the sentence.

### Collective nouns

Nouns such as committee, family, staff, police and government take a singular verb or pronoun when thought of as a single unit, but a plural verb and pronoun when thought of as a collection of individuals.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| The staff of ECMWF **is** international (single unit) |
| ECMWF staff **are** welcome to attend the seminar (collection of individuals) |

### Past participles as adjectives

Avoid using the past participles of verbs (e.g. *obtained, needed, derived*) before nouns. A better formulation is to place the participle **after** the noun, or to use an adjective **before** the noun.

| ✅ | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| The **necessary** parameters will be added. | The **needed** parameters will be added. |
| The measurements **obtained** were recorded. | The **obtained** measurements were recorded. |

### Nouns used as adjectives

Plural nouns used as adjectives become singular.

| ✅ | Exception |
| --- | --- |
| dissemination of **products** -> **product** dissemination | a two-**thirds** majority |
| wind at 10 **metres** -> 10-**metre** wind |  |
| contract for four **years** -> four-**year** contract |  |
| a delay of two **seconds** -> a two-**second** delay |  |
| scalability of **codes** -> **code** scalability |  |

### Sensitive/gender-neutral language

> [!NOTE]
> This section will be updated to reflect changes applied in ECMWF policies/regulations.

Try to avoid language that may be perceived as sexist. For example, there are a number of alternatives for the following sentence.

**❌** If a staff member is ill, he must inform his department director.

1. Rephrase the sentence to use the plural, where possible. This is the preferred option. **If staff members are ill, they must inform their department director.**
2. Use “he or she” and “his or her”. Avoid using “s/he”, “(s)he”, “his/her”, etc. **If a staff member is ill, he or she must inform his or her department director.**
3. They and their are often used with a singular meaning in informal contexts, but should be avoided in formal writing. **If a staff member is ill, they must inform their department director.**

Take care also with titles, job descriptions and other terms that may not include both sexes. For example, use **Chair** not Chairman and **person-hour** not man-hour.

### Correspondence

Dear Sir/Madam (unnamed person) -> sign off “Yours faithfully”

Dear Mr Smith (named person) -> sign off “Yours sincerely”

### Reference to web content

To refer to information on web pages, use **at** not from or on.

| ✅ | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| The forms can be downloaded **at** http://www … | The forms can be downloaded **from/on** http://www … |
| Visit us **at** http://www… | Visit us **on** http://www… |

Include the “http://” in the address. If the address starts with “www”, we can omit “http://”. However, if a number of addresses are listed together, be consistent in the use of prefixes.

### Word choice and spelling

<details>
<summary>Allow/permit</summary>

Avoid using allow/permit followed by the “to infinitive”.

| ❌ | Note |
| --- | --- |
| The upgrade **allows/permits to access** data much more quickly. |  |
| ✅ |  |
| The upgrade allows/permits **users** **to access** data much more quickly. | allow/permit + somebody/something to do something |
| The upgrade allows/permits **faster access to data**. | allow/permit + something |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Recommend/suggest</summary>

| ❌ | Note |
| --- | --- |
| It is **recommended/suggested to** **increase** the computer budget. | Avoid using recommend/suggest followed by the “to infinitive” |
| The Committee **recommended/suggested to increase** the computer budget. | Avoid using recommend/suggest followed by the “to infinitive” |
| ✅ |  |
| The Committee **recommended/suggested increasing** the computer budget. | recommend/suggest + -ing |
| The Committee **recommended the organisation to increase** the computer budget. | recommend + somebody + to do something (cannot be used with suggest) |
| The Committee **recommended/suggested that** the organisation **increase** the computer budget. | recommend/suggest + that + subjunctive |
| **It is recommended/suggested that** the computer budget **be increased**. | If you don’t want to specify who is making the recommendation/suggestion, you can use the passive. |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Fewer/less</summary>

Use **fewer** for countable plural nouns and **less** for uncountable amounts.

| ✅ | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| In practice there are **fewer** observations than variables in the model. | In practice there are **less** observations than variables in the model. |
| This technique provides **less** information. | This technique provides **fewer** information. |
| There were **fewer** participants than last year. | There were **less** participants than last year. |

</details>

## Style guidelines

### Reference to ECMWF

On first mention, write out the name of the organisation in full, with shorter forms in brackets if you intend to use them later in the text.

| ✅ | Note |
| --- | --- |
| The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts |  |
| ECMWF | “ECMWF” is preferable to “the Centre”. |
| the Centre |  |

The organisation as a whole is viewed as a singular body, taking a singular verb and pronoun. Use *it* not *they* to talk about ECMWF.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts **provides** medium-range and seasonal forecasts. **It** is funded by **its** Member States. |

In some more informal contexts (such as the Newsletter), it may be appropriate to adopt a more personal tone and use the terms *we* and *our* rather than *it* and *its*.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| To improve **our** severe weather warning capabilities at extended ranges, **we** have developed a highly skilled seasonal forecasting capability and introduced **our** monthly forecasting system. |

### Spelling

Use British not American English. Refer to the Concise Oxford English Dictionary (OED) for reference (copies are available in the Centre Library). The OED can be accessed online at <https://www.oed.com/>. Where variants exist for a given word, take the first entry.

**Exception:** In British English, certain terms may be spelled with “s” or “z”, such as *realise/realize, optimise/optimize* and *organisation/organization*. The Concise OED gives the “ize” variant as the first entry. **ECMWF generally uses the “ise” variant**.

In British English, you sometimes have to double a single consonant when adding *-ed* or *-ing*, for example, travel -> travelled, travelling.

### Punctuation

<details>
<summary>Space</summary>

- There is no space before question marks, exclamation marks, inverted commas, colons, semicolons, etc. or after currency symbols.
- Insert a single (not double) space after full stops, colons, semicolons, question marks and exclamation marks.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Apostrophe</summary>

Use smart (curly) apostrophes not straight ones (’ not '). In Word, choose *File* -> *Options* -> *Proofing* -> *AutoCorrect Options*. On the *AutoFormat As You Type* tab, select the checkbox to replace straight quotes with smart quotes.

#### Contractions

Apostrophes indicate contractions, such as *don’t* (do not), *can’t* (cannot), *it’s* (it is). Avoid these forms in formal writing.

#### It’s versus its

The only time *it’s* needs an apostrophe is when it is standing for *it is*. There is no apostrophe when it is possessive (like *his/her/its*).

#### Names

When a name ends in “s”, add apostrophe s (’s) to the end of the word. E.g. James's book.

#### Plurals

No apostrophes in the plurals of abbreviations or acronyms (*VDUs* not *VDU’s, 1980s* not *1980's*).

#### Possessive

The apostrophe goes before the final “s” when the owner is singular and after the final “s” when the owner is plural.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| The Centre’s annual budget (one Centre) |
| The auditor’s report (one auditor) |
| The auditors’ report (more than one auditor) |
| Member States’ contributions (more than one MS) |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Brackets</summary>

If the text inside the brackets is a full sentence, place punctuation inside the brackets.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Colon</summary>

#### Headings

No colons at the end of headings.

#### Introductory phrases

Use a colon at the end of an introductory phrase before a bulleted or numbered list.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Comma</summary>

- **Introductory phrases.** Use a comma after an introductory phrase or clause. You can omit the comma if the phrase is very short and if no ambiguity results.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | Based on information received from Member States, the Council expects … |
  | In 2002, we will investigate … **or** In 2002 we will investigate … |
- Within sentences, use commas to isolate words such as *however, therefore, consequently, for example*.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | It is, therefore, considered appropriate that … |
- See also [Commas in relative clauses](#commas-in-relative-clauses).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Dash</summary>

A dash is longer than a hyphen and has a different function. Dashes are used to set off additional information and to show ranges. They are informal and it is better in formal writing to use brackets or commas to set off information.

Keyboard shortcut: Ctrl + Num- (the minus sign on the number pad)

#### Giving additional information

Use an **en-dash** with a space before and after to include additional information or comments (functioning like brackets).

| ✅ |
| --- |
| The approved budget was – as expected – higher than the previous year. |

#### Ranges

Use an **en-dash** without spaces, not a hyphen, to indicate ranges. In ranges, dashes replace the word *to*.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| 1980–1990 |
| The period January–March |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Full stop</summary>

#### Headings

No full stops in headings.

#### Titles

No full stops for titles of people.

| ✅ | Exception |
| --- | --- |
| Mr, Mrs, Miss, Ms, Dr | Prof. |

#### Names

Use a full stop and space after initials.

| ✅ | Note |
| --- | --- |
| Mr J. H. Smith | May be written with or without punctuation in correspondence. Aim for consistency within a given piece of correspondence. |

#### Footnotes

Start with a capital and end in a full stop.

#### Abbreviations

No full stop if the abbreviation ends in the final letter of the word (contraction). Otherwise, use a full stop.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| department -> dept (no full stop) |
| paragraph -> para. (full stop) |

#### Acronyms

No full stops in abbreviations and acronyms made up of capitals (VDU not V.D.U.).

#### Sentences ending in abbreviations

If a sentence ends with an abbreviation that has a full stop (such as *etc.*), don’t add another full stop.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hyphen</summary>

Hyphens join words together and show which words belong together in cases where there could be ambiguity, for example, *higher-resolution, ten-day forecast*. In Word, use non-breaking hyphens (Ctrl + Shift + -) to keep hyphenated words together on the same line.

#### Fractions

Hyphenate fractions when written in words.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| Two-thirds of the members present voted. |
| a two-thirds majority (adjective) |

#### Compound adjectives

Hyphenate compound adjectives if they come before the noun. If they come after the noun, no hyphen is required.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| long-term strategy **but** strategy for the long term |
| real-time analysis **but** an analysis in real time |
| ten-year period **but** a period of ten years |

#### Adverbs modifying adjectives

If the adverb ends in *-ly*, there is not usually a hyphen.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| unusually high winds |
| suitably qualified applicants |

#### Ranges

Use dashes not hyphens to indicate ranges (dates, amounts, etc.). See “Dash” section above.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Quotation marks</summary>

#### General

Use smart (curly) quotation marks not straight ones (“…” not "…").

#### Single or double

Use double quotes at the start and end of a quoted section. Use single quotation marks for quotes within quotes.

#### Position

If quoting a complete sentence, use a colon before the quote and place punctuation inside the quotation marks. Otherwise, place punctuation outside the quotation marks.

| ✅ | Hint |
| --- | --- |
| One of the goals stated in the ECMWF strategy is “to contribute towards the optimisation of the Global Observing System”. | ~~*ECMWF* tab -> *Apply ECMWF styles -> Apply quotation* indents highlighted text and sets it in italics. This is useful if you want to set off a large quote from the rest of the text.~~ <br/> Do not use italics for quotes that are part of a normal sentence. |
| She concluded: “The new and enlarged premises will enhance ECMWF’s future in the UK and I pledge our ongoing support for you and your future development.” |  |

#### Reference to documents

When referring to another document, quote the exact name in double quotation marks and capitalise the title as it appears in the document.

#### Block quotes

For longer quotes, you can set off the quote from the rest of the text. No need for quotation marks in this case. In Word and on the ECMWF website, styles are defined for displaying block quotes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Semicolon</summary>

#### Linking sentences

Semicolons can be used to link two sentences without a conjunction (and, but, or, etc.).

#### Complex lists

If the items in a list already contain commas, it can be helpful to separate the items with semicolons rather than commas.

#### Bulleted and numbered lists

If the items in a bulleted list follow on from the introductory sentence, end each item with a semicolon and end the final item with a full stop.

</details>

### Capitalisation

Avoid using capitals for emphasis. If you want to stress a particular term, use other formatting such as italics, bold or quotation marks, but avoid underlining.

<details>
<summary>Capitals in English</summary>

English uses capitals in the following places:

| ✅ |
| --- |
| Proper names, including the names of specific projects and initiatives |
| Trademarks, product names |
| Upper-case abbreviations/acronyms (ECMWF, VDU) |
| Days of the week, months of the year (**not seasons**) |
| Countries and related words (France, French) |

Avoid using capitals for emphasis. If you want to stress a particular term, use other formatting such as italics, bold or quotation marks, but avoid underlining.

| ✅ | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| implement and validate “feature-tracking” software | implement and validate Feature-Tracking software |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Abbreviations</summary>

Do not capitalise terms that you are abbreviating unless they would normally be capitalised, even if the abbreviation is in capitals.

| ✅ | Note |
| --- | --- |
| local area network (LAN) |  |
| invitation to tender (ITT) | *Invitation to Tender* is capitalised when referring to a specific ITT, followed by the number, for example, Invitation to Tender No: ECMWF/2008/… |
| leaf area index (LAI) |  |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Headings and captions</summary>

- Capitalise only the first word and proper names in headings and subheadings, and in captions for graphs, tables and diagrams (sentence-style capitalisation).

  | ✅ | ❌ |
  | --- | --- |
  | Overview of computing systems | Overview of Computing Systems |
- Proper names remain capitalised when they appear in titles or headings.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | Meeting of new **Co-operating States** |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Countries and states</summary>

- The names of countries and states, and related nationality words, are always capitalised (*Germany, France, Sweden, Europe, European, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands*).
- In body text, the term *State* is usually capitalised when referring to a political community or nation, but *country* is usually lower case.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | Some **States** previously paid an SAC when becoming Co-operating States. |
  | … decided that the SAC payable by **States** becoming Member States should be determined as the amount which these **States** would have to pay had they been Member States … |
  | The Kingdom of Morocco is the first African **country** to conclude a co-operation agreement with ECMWF. |

</details>

<details>
<summary>People</summary>

- Use capitals for job titles referring to a specific person or position.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | Check with the **Receptionist** for details. |
  | The **Director-General** will send out a memo. |
  | Reports should be submitted to the **Director of Administration** or the **Director of Research**. |
- General job titles not referring to a specific person do not need capitals.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | Forms are available from **departmental secretaries**. |
  | The **directors** of the companies concerned will meet to discuss the issue. |
  | You must obtain permission from the relevant **department director**. |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Organisations</summary>

- The names of specific organisations and institutions are proper names and should be capitalised. Always check how an organisation refers to itself.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | The **Met Office** plays a key role on the international stage. |
  | Students from the **University of Reading**. |
- When referring to a type of organisation in general, such as a university or government, capitals are not necessary.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | As a **national meteorological service** (NMS), the Met Office relies heavily on international co-operation. |
  | Students from **universities** are welcome to visit the Centre. |
- Names of departments and sections at ECMWF have initial capital letters. Refer to the [Organigram](../../../../Intranet/Intranet%20Home/The%20organisation%20and%20teams/Organigram.md).

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | Administration Department |
  | Personnel Section |
- Organisations and companies are viewed as singular entities, taking a singular verb.

  | ✅ | ❌ |
  | --- | --- |
  | Microsoft **has** developed a software package to…. | Microsoft **have** developed a software package to…. |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference to documents</summary>

- When referring to another document, quote the exact name in double quotation marks and capitalise the title as it appears in the document. This signals to the reader that another document exists for reference and enables them to find the intended reference because they have the correct document name.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | The Council adopted the “ECMWF Strategy 2006–2015” (quote the exact document name here). |
  | The Council considered the “Four-year programme of activities 20XX–20XX”. |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Capitals with shorter forms</summary>

- If you have mentioned a noun that is capitalised, such as an organisation, group or document, and subsequently use a shorter form or different term to refer back to it, it is not normally necessary to use capitals, especially if no ambiguity would result. This is a grey area, so aim for consistency within documents. Generally speaking, the more official or permanent the thing is, the more likely it is to be capitalised.
- For permanent/long-term groups and organisations, use capitals for the short forms.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | the Advisory Committee of Co-operating States -> the Committee |
  | the TAC Subgroup on the High Performance Computing Facility -> the Subgroup |
- In many cases, if an event or document has already been clearly identified, and no ambiguity would result, it is preferable to use lower case to refer back to it.

  | ✅ | Note |
  | --- | --- |
  | The **workshop** was a huge success. | Referring to a specific workshop already named in full. |
  | The Council considered the proposals outlined in the **four-year programme**. | Referring to “Four-year programme of activities XXXX–XXXX”, already clearly named in text. |
- In some cases, especially legal documents and contracts, or where ambiguity may result, it is usual to specify in brackets after first mention the shorter form that will be used throughout the rest of the text (as for abbreviations).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Legislation and regulations</summary>

The general rule is to capitalise specific references and use lower case for general references. Capitals are often used within legal documents, such as co-operation agreements or contracts, to define the specific parties and concepts.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| General: The Centre has concluded **co-operation agreements** with 13 States. |
| Specific (within an agreement): This **Agreement** shall enter into force on signature. |

Capitalise terms such as *Decision*, *Annex* and *Article* (followed by a number) if they refer to specific ECMWF documents or regulations.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| The regulations are laid out in **Article 24**. |

#### ECMWF Convention and other regulations

When referring to or quoting from the Convention, write the numbers and letters for different sections without spaces.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| Article 6(1)(e) |

#### Draft, provisional, adopted

These terms are not capitalised in body text.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| Please find attached the provisional agenda for the meeting. |
| The draft amendment will be posted tomorrow. |

#### Reference to document parts

When referring to a specific chapter or section (followed by a number) within the same document, use capitals. It is not necessary to capitalise the following terms: *paragraph, page, point, line*. If you are not naming a specific section, use lower case.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| the information given in **Chapter 2** |
| the graph in **Section 4** |
| the graph in the **next section** |
| Refer to the table in **Annex 3.** |
| See the plan on **page 56**. |

</details>

### Numbers, time and dates

<details>
<summary>Words or figures</summary>

- Write ordinal and cardinal numbers up to and including ten in words. Write numbers from 11 upwards in figures.

  | ✅ | ❌ |
  | --- | --- |
  | **four-year** programme | **4-year** programme |
  | was forecast **eight days** in advance | was forecast **8 days** in advance |
  | This network of points is then repeated at **60 levels** in the vertical. | This network of points is then repeated at **sixty levels** in the vertical. |

- If one sentence contains numbers below and above ten, write all the numbers in figures.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | The time range **10** to **30** days is probably still short enough that the atmosphere retains some memory of its initial state. |

- Avoid beginning a sentence with a figure; write the number in words or reformulate the sentence.
- Use figures for references to pages, chapters and sections.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | See page **5** |
  | Refer to Chapter **6** |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Time</summary>

- Use the 24-hour clock format in preference to the 12-hour clock with a.m. and p.m.
- Do not add *hr*, *hrs* or *o’clock* after the time.
- With the 24-hour clock system, use a colon not a full stop to separate hours and minutes.

  | ✅ | ❌ |
  | --- | --- |
  | 14:00 | 14.00 hrs |

- If you do need to use the 12-hour clock, write *a.m.* and *p.m.* (lower case, full stops, space between time and abbreviation, e.g. 2.30 p.m.). This format uses a full stop on the line rather than a colon.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | 2 p.m. |

- In technical texts, use UTC in preference to GMT.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | Based on the EPS forecast from 00 UTC on 15 January … |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Dates</summary>

#### Long and short date formats

In text, use the long date format, writing out the month in full. This will avoid confusion with the US date format, where the month comes first in the short format (05/10/2007 = 10 May not 5 October in the US). Use the short format in tables.

| ✅  Long: (day,) date month year | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| 27 June 2008 | 27<sup>th</sup> June 2008 |
| Friday, 27 June 2008 | Friday the 27<sup>th</sup> of June 2008 |
| ✅  Short: dd.mm.yyyy | ❌ |

#### Ranges

Use a dash without spaces to indicate a time span. (Keyboard shortcut: Ctrl + Num- minus sign on number pad). If you introduce the range with a word like “from” or “between”, don’t use a dash to complete the range. Use “to” or “and” instead.

| ✅ | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| the period 1980–2006 |  |

<summary>Currencies</summary>

#### Currency names and symbols

- In text, if the actual amount is not stated, write the name of the currency in full (pounds sterling, euro). Named currencies take **lower case**.

  | ✅ | ❌ |
  | --- | --- |
  | All amounts are given in pounds sterling. | All amounts are given in Pounds Sterling. |
  | The account shall be managed in euros. | The account shall be managed in Euros/EUR. |

- If the amount is written in words, use the currency name. If the amount is written in figures, use the symbol or abbreviation. The sign always comes before the amount, and there is no space between the sign and the amount.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | three hundred pounds |
  | twenty euros |
  | £300 |
  | €3,100.80 |

- Confusion may arise if a symbol is used by more than one country (for example, the dollar sign ($) is used for other currencies besides the US dollar). In such cases, consider using ISO codes throughout, or clarify which currency is meant on first mention. The most important thing is to be consistent within a document.

#### Common currency codes and signs

| Currency name | ISO code | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pound sterling, pounds sterling | GBP | £ |
| euro, euros | EUR | € |
| US dollar, US dollars | USD | $ |

#### Ranges

When giving a range of amounts, use an en dash not a hyphen. Keyboard shortcut: Ctrl + Num- (minus sign on number pad). It is not necessary to repeat the currency code/symbol.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| This will require funding of GBP 300–400 |

#### Millions and billions

In body text, write out *million* and *billion* in full, with a space between the figure and the word. In tables and diagrams, the words *million* and *billion* may be abbreviated to *m* and *bn*.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| The cost involved is estimated to be in the region of EUR 3 million–4 million |

<summary>Units of measure and symbols</summary>

In general, use the International System of Units (SI – Système International). Details on the conventions for writing the names and symbols of units of measure can be found in the SI brochure (<http://www.bipm.org/en/si/si_brochure/>), especially Chapter 5.

#### Units of measurement

Full names are written lower case and have normal plurals.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| kilogram, kilograms |
| metre, metres |

#### Symbols and abbreviations

- If you use a symbol or abbreviation for a unit of measure (e.g. **kg** for **kilogram**), write the number in figures not words. Insert a non-breaking space between the figure and the abbreviation.

  | ✅ | ❌ |
  | --- | --- |
  | 6 kg | six kg |

- If you spell out the unit of measure in words, the number may be written in words or figures.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | 6 kilograms |
  | six kilograms |
- Use lower case for measures such as kg, km and mph (refer also to “Degrees in temperatures” below). Symbols do not have full stops or plurals.

  | ✅ | ❌ |
  | --- | --- |
  | 15 kg | 15 kgs, 15kg, 15 KG |
  | 100 mph | 100 m.p.h, 100mph |

- For ranges, repeat the unit to avoid ambiguity.

  | ✅ |
  | --- |
  | For spatial resolutions between 40 km and 120 km. |

#### Degrees in coordinates

There is no space between the number and the degree sign.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| 50°N |

#### Degrees in temperatures

Express temperatures in figures not words. Leave no space between the figure and the degree sign.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| 30°C |

The abbreviation for kelvin is K (no degree sign).

### Abbreviations and acronyms

Try to keep abbreviations to a minimum. If you do need to abbreviate, write out the term in full on first mention, followed by the abbreviation in brackets. Thereafter, use the abbreviation. Don’t use full stops in upper-case abbreviations or in acronyms.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| All data, analyses and forecast fields available at ECMWF are stored in the Meteorological Archival and Retrieval System (MARS). Use MARS from your browser. |

When you write out the term in full, only use capitals if the term would normally be written with capitals (proper names, names of organisations and initiatives, etc.).

| ✅ | ❌ |
| --- | --- |
| ECMWF recently replaced the automated tape library (ATL). | ECMWF recently replaced the Automated Tape Library (ATL). |
| A local area network (LAN) is a computer network covering a small geographic area, like a home, office, or group of buildings. | A Local Area Network (LAN) is a computer network covering a small geographic area, like a home, office, or group of buildings. |
| Introduction of a lake model and seasonal leaf area index (LAI) | Introduction of a lake model and seasonal Leaf Area Index (LAI) |

Try not to begin a sentence with an abbreviation (except ECMWF).

Write capitalised abbreviations without full stops (UK not U.K.).

Write the plurals of abbreviations and acronyms without apostrophes (VDUs not VDU’s).

Use a full stop if the abbreviation does not end in the final letter of the word. If the final letter is included in the abbreviation (contraction), don’t use a full stop.

| ✅ |
| --- |
| department -> dept (no full stop) |
| paragraph -> para. (full stop) |

### Lists and tables

<details>
<summary>Lists in body text</summary>

- Use commas to separate items listed in a sentence. There is no comma before the *and* before the final item. If you introduce a list using *e.g.* do not add *etc.* at the end. Use **either** *e.g.* *such as* **or** *etc.*

  | ✅ |  |
  | --- | --- |
  | This guide contains information about spelling, grammar, capitalisation **and** abbreviations. | This guide contains information about spelling, grammar, capitalisation **and** abbreviations. |

- For more complicated lists, separate the items using semicolons. Use *and* before the final item.

  | ✅ |  |
  | --- | --- |
  | ECMWF’s principal objectives are the development of numerical methods for medium-range and seasonal weather **forecasting;** the preparation, on a regular basis, of medium-range and long-range weather forecasts for distribution to the meteorological services of the Member **States;** scientific and technical research directed to the improvement of these **forecasts;** **and** the collection and storage of appropriate meteorological data. | ECMWF’s principal objectives are the development of numerical methods for medium-range and seasonal weather **forecasting;** the preparation, on a regular basis, of medium-range and long-range weather forecasts for distribution to the meteorological services of the Member **States;** scientific and technical research directed to the improvement of these **forecasts;** **and** the collection and storage of appropriate meteorological data. |

</details>

<details>
<summary>Bulleted and numbered lists</summary>

Beware of overusing lists, and bear in mind the following points:

- Introduce the list with an introductory sentence or phrase. Try to use a complete sentence where possible. End the sentence or phrase with a colon or full stop.
- Avoid lists that complete the introductory sentence, as the reader has to keep referring back to the start of the sentence.
- Avoid lists where the sentence continues after the listed items as they are difficult to read and translate. Try to rephrase the introductory sentence instead.
- Aim to be consistent in the grammatical structures and wording you use in the list. For example, begin each item with a verb, or each item with a noun. Check that each item follows on logically from the introductory sentence/phrase.
- Have at least two items in the list. Otherwise, formulate the list as a normal sentence.
- Use bullets if the sequence of the items in the list is not important. If the sequence is important, use numbers.
- Don’t write “and” or “or” at the end of the penultimate item.

#### Punctuation in bulleted and numbered lists

**If the items in the list are not complete sentences** and do not follow on from the introductory phrase, begin **each** item with a capital letter and do not use any end punctuation.

> Summary of principal changes:
> - New physics/dynamics coupling in the semi-Lagrangian context
> - New soil moisture and temperature analysis
> - Use of Meteosat high density winds

**If one or more items in the list contain a full sentence**, begin **each** item with a capital letter and end **each** item with a full stop. Where possible, turn all items into sentences.

> New changes to the general structure of the programme have been introduced:
> - The technical summary (former chapter 2) has been removed.
> - A list of the main actions is now provided as a conclusion of Chapter 3.
> - Chapters 4 and 5 have been reorganised and shifted. Chapter 4 now clearly deals with computing systems and Chapter 5 with external relations and outreach.

**If the items follow on from the introductory sentence**, begin each item with a lower-case letter and end each item with a semicolon. End the final item with a full stop. It can be difficult to read and translate this construction, so avoid them if you can by rephrasing the introductory sentence or by presenting the information as a normal sentence or paragraph instead.

> The only exceptions will be:
> - papers dealing with appeals;
> - papers dealing with selection boards;
> - documents concerning individual staff members, at those staff members’ request.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Tables and figures</summary>

- Capitalise only the first word in table headings.
- Within tables, capitalise only the first word and proper names in each heading for a column or row.
- Keep abbreviations to a minimum and explain any symbols or abbreviations used in the table or figure.
- If a document has more than one table, name or number the tables. Place table headings above the table. Place captions for figures, graphs and diagrams below the figure in question.

</details>
